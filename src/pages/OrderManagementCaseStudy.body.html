Product Context

The company is a large B2B investment firm serving advisors, banks, and broker‑dealers. Order management is the backstage tool that allows investment professionals at these firms to place and manage trades in the stock market. It's the system where big orders start as drafts, get checked against strict rules, and move through various states until they're fully complete. It keeps everything in one clear view so these professional users can move fast, stay compliant, and spend their energy on investment decisions for their own clients rather than the logistics of trade execution.

tl;dr

Context

This workflow sat at the center of institutional trades, where every click carried real financial and regulatory risk. In a fast "modernize to cloud" push, design was mostly seen as a way to freshen aging tools. My first concept was a feature‑rich, dashboard‑style view, but early research showed it added complexity and pulled traders away from the tasks that mattered most.

Approach

I shifted to an essentials‑first approach, using addition through subtraction to focus on only the critical tasks and make them feel simple, approachable, and automatic.

Outcome

The result was a streamlined workflow that felt clearer and more trustworthy for traders, plus a new component and layout pattern that scaled cleanly across multiple products on the platform.

Where Order Management sits

At the center of every trade.

No matter where an order starts—on-platform tickets, external integrations, or manual entry—it eventually flows through this surface. Drafts, compliance checks, monitoring, and post‑trade actions all meet here, which is why getting it right mattered: a misstep on this screen ripples across every team and system that touches a trade.

The Legacy Experience

The system users lived in

Here's what users were working with every day: three compounding pain points that made a high-stakes workflow harder than it needed to be.

Filters and order details both expanded vertically, collapsing the orders table from opposite ends. Users couldn't filter, review details, and see their list at the same time — forcing constant trade-offs in a workflow where all three mattered.

There was no visible way to select orders — bulk actions were buried behind shift-clicks and right-click menus. In a workflow built around acting on multiple orders at once, there was no visual affordance for this critical action.

The send flow offered no visibility into what was being sent or why errors existed — just vague options and a confirmation screen with nothing actionable. Sending orders is the highest-stakes moment in the workflow, and users were doing it blind.

Hypothesis & Research

Testing the dashboard hypothesis

We began with a hypothesis: if Order Management is an end-to-end workflow, a central dashboard might help users move more fluidly across it. Early signals from incident reports and usability issues suggested fragmentation was real, so we explored whether a more consolidated view could solve it.

How I pressure-tested the idea

To test the direction, I ran exploratory interviews with roughly 20 participants across clearing and custody, then used rough concept feedback to get an early read on the dashboard idea. This wasn't a polished concept test — it was a gut check on whether the direction actually matched the way people worked.

What users actually needed

The research told a different story. Users didn't want a dashboard — they wanted the list, made faster. Their core need wasn't to see everything at once, but to move quickly between the order list and the few details needed to act without losing their place.

01

The list was the work.

Users spent most of their time scanning and working through orders, not navigating a broad control center.

02

Details needed to be closer.

Users wanted order-level context and alerts on the same surface, without opening a separate page.

03

Reliability beat flash.

In time-sensitive work, predictability and speed mattered more than a sleek, feature-rich experience.

The reframe

The zooming users needed was between the list and individual order details — check a limit price, review an error, confirm a status — not between the list and a full account-level dashboard.

"It works like a Honda Accord. Not flashy, but reliable."
— Senior Adviser, Custody firm

That framing clarified something important: reliability wasn't just a preference. In a time-sensitive trading workflow, slow, broken, or inaccessible tools carry real cost.

Design Principles

Rules for every decision.

Research and concept feedback shaped a set of guiding principles for the redesign.

01

Protect the table

The order list never shrinks or hides.

02

Go horizontal

Free up vertical space; move supporting UI into a horizontal layer.

03

Make configuration effortless

Setup should feel almost automatic, not like managing a settings page.

04

Surface relevant actions

The next step should be obvious from the state of the row.

05

One surface, one job

This screen is for monitoring and acting on orders — not deep account analysis.

New Design

A surface built to protect the list

Wanted a pill-based filter pattern, but the design system couldn't support new components. I composed a horizontal, collapsible filter panel from existing atoms instead. Active filter pills above the table show at a glance what's included or excluded.

Rebalanced the table for a clean left‑to‑right scan: key signals first, status and alerts aligned by column, and hover/tap affordances that keep actions close to where work happens.

Brought essential order details into a side panel so traders can inspect, fix, and confirm without losing their place in the list.

Surfacing the right next step.

Checkboxes plus a "Select…" dropdown with counts (on page, matching filters, all buys, all sells, etc.) turn bulk actions into clear, informed choices before sending.

A verification step that reads back the action in plain language, highlights risk, and gives a clear escape hatch before anything goes out the door.

Subtle links and toasts connect this surface to related tools and states, so traders always know what happened and where to go next.

Constraints

Working within reality

Every decision had to reconcile four things: AG Grid's capabilities, our design system, backend limits, and strict accessibility standards. The gap between ideal and shippable defined both the MVP and the backlog.

AG Grid filters vs. accessibility — Native in‑column filters failed accessibility and clashed with our system components, so I composed a persistent horizontal filter panel from existing atoms instead.

Live quotes and logos — Security logos and live quotes were technically feasible, but loading live or even recent data across multiple rows created a noticeable performance cost. We kept the design ready and deferred the feature.

Account‑level data for every trade — Showing full account context for each row would have overloaded the backend. We chose progressive disclosure instead and moved full account views to a later phase.

Saved views — Saved views required backend persistence we didn't have in scope. We still designed for that future state and tracked it alongside in‑column filtering and other AG Grid enhancements for post‑MVP.

Outcome

What shipped & what it changed

For users

  • Filter without losing the list.
  • See issues at a glance with row‑level indicators.
  • Select with clear, visible affordances for bulk actions.
  • Review with full error transparency before sending.
  • Track confirmation and execution states in one place.
  • Configure views to match daily workflow.

For the product & org

  • A new horizontal filter pattern became a reusable platform component.
  • The work established a research‑driven playbook for modernization projects.
  • We left a clear roadmap from the "ideal" experience into later releases when backend and data constraints caught up.

Pilot Feedback

What we learned after launch

We piloted the new surface with users who had access to both the legacy and redesigned experiences. That gave us a clear read on where the changes landed and where the remaining friction still lived.

Tab clarity

Sent and Draft states weren't distinct enough; some users missed the state change. This pushed us to strengthen the tab treatment and rely less on subtle cues.

Details interaction

The drawer pattern interrupted grid scrolling. That feedback opened the door to a split-panel pattern that kept the list and details active at the same time.

Error scanning

Scanning for issues across large order sets still required too much visual effort. We used that signal to explore stronger row-level indicators and smarter defaults for error views.

What's Next

Where this surface can grow

Some high-value ideas were intentionally deferred—not because they lacked value, but because they depended on data, backend support, or more time in the wild. The next phase is about making the same surface smarter without making it busier.

Saved views

Remember the filters, columns, and sort states that matter most to each user so the surface opens in a familiar, ready-to-work state.

Smarter filtering

Evolve from a persistent panel toward lighter inline or context-aware filters as accessibility and system support improve.

Deeper account context

Pull positions, balances, and other account signals into the flow only when they're needed to make a decision—never by default.

Context-aware assistance

Use what we know about the active order set to surface next-best actions, warnings, or checks without flooding the screen.

Reflection

Why the simpler answer was the right one

There's a version of this case study where the punchline is a brand-new dashboard. Instead, what shipped was a better version of what users already trusted: modernized filtering, a layout that respects how people actually scan a list, order details you can reach without losing your place, and a send flow that builds confidence instead of anxiety. Exploring the heavier, feature-rich direction first gave us the evidence to walk back to the simple one with conviction. For me, that's the core of the work: use research to cut through "more" as the default answer, protect the parts of the system people already rely on, and ship only the changes that make their day feel calmer, clearer, and more automatic.
